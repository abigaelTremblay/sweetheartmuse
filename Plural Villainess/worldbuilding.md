## 1. Magic Rules (Summary & Additions)

### 1.1 Mantles

**[Canon]**
- Donning takes **a silent prayer**. Well-practised prayers shorten until merely thinking the ancestor's name in a worshipful headspace is enough.
- Mantles are spiritual: a ghost of the ancestor melds with the wearer. **Stone and walls do not block** them or their pressure.
- Broken weapons are completed by the ancestor's spectral weapon. Lost limbs/eyes are replaced by ghostly ones while the Mantle holds.
- Regeneration follows the **"witnessed wounds"** model (below). The earlier snapshot-revert idea was dropped. Memories are never restored or reverted.
- Colour is visible while Mantled. Since Mantles aren't worn constantly, a rival's colour is known only from reputation or a witnessed fight.
- Anyone within ~100 m feels faint pressure from an active Mantle ("Mantle up at your own peril"), stronger per additional Mantle. The gradient is perceptible roughly every 10–15 m.
- **Multiple people may wear the same ancestor at once.** More wearers do **not** make the Mantle stronger; strength depends only on each wearer's devotion, ideological fit and lifestyle fit. Level playing field.
- **Non-warrior Mantles exist** (craft, scholarly, spell-carrying). They grant perfect execution, not judgment; the wearer must still decide how to apply it.
- New Mantles form two ways: Myth (a person famous enough on their own merit) or mastering 2+ Mantles into a new style. Fresh, merit-born Mantles are weaker and less refined (no legacy layered on them) but **easier to master**, making them stepping stones for "cultivator" hermits. Lifespans are normal; a hermit's new Mantle is a sacrifice of their life for their legacy.
- The ~100 m strain limit is central: everyone wants Mantle power, but the opportunity cost of a slot precludes others.
- Mantles assist pregnancy: healing/revives apply to the mother. Holding a Mantle long-term in the weeks before birth can strengthen the baby, but leaves the household vulnerable (one extra Mantle nearby strains her off within ~15 min).
- Gods are Mantles like any other, capped by the same rules. Only their reverence and use differ.

**[Canon] Regeneration model: "witnessed wounds"**
- The Mantle heals **only harm it witnessed while worn**, and **the ancestor judges what counts as harm**. Healing is the ghost limb or ghost flesh becoming real flesh, which fits the canon ghost-limb replacement.
- Pregnancy, birth, ageing, growth, training gains, weight and tans are **not wounds**, so they are never undone. Tearing and blood loss from birth *are* wounds.
- Harm already present when donning (old wounds, illness, poison taken earlier) wasn't witnessed, so it isn't healed. The "don healthy" ritual culture survives.
- **The ancestor's understanding sets the limits.** A warrior recognises cuts, burns, broken bones and blood loss, but may not understand poison, disease or childbirth. A healer or midwife ancestor does.
  - **The confinement Mantle must be an ancestor who gave birth**, or birth injuries go unhealed. Choosing a confinement ancestor is a serious family decision, and motherly ancestors have high status.
  - Poisoners pick poisons the opponent's ancestor wouldn't understand, such as a slow sickness or something unknown in the ancestor's era. **Old ancestors don't understand new weapons** (gunpowder, new toxins), which matters a lot in a 19th-century setting.
  - Having the ancestor misjudge or refuse to heal something is a story device; heretical surgeries could exploit it.
- Scars only form from unmantled wounds, or wounds the ancestor didn't recognise. Scars are marks of commoners, the unprotected, or honour (a deliberate unmantled duel).
- **Torture leaves no marks, but the mind doesn't reset.** Trauma builds up unseen (the eldest daughter: "pushed and killed within a Mantle," physically fine, a shell inside).

**[Canon] Alternative: "heal toward the self-image"**, as **heretical lore only**: possible only by mixing Myth with a Mantle
- Pros: body-horror and identity themes; dysmorphia would heal badly; it could speed up the 2nd son's transition.
- Cons: it blurs Mantles (safe, ancestral) with Myth (belief reshaping you). Mantles stop being the "pure" path, trauma survivors heal worse (unfair to an equal-footing system), and vanity becomes a combat stat.
- **Better as a hybrid:** a Mystic who taps Myth *while Mantled* can steer healing toward their self-image. It's powerful, heretical, and a mark the Inquisition hunts for (people who heal "wrong" or heal *better* than before). It's a quiet, dangerous way for the 2nd son's transition to speed up, and a secret the Lord (a Myth expert) would know.

**[Canon] Silent prayer and true names**
- Experienced wearers need only *think* the name, which protects the true name.
- Spoken prayer is easier and faster for beginners, so leaking a name is a mistake of youth or panic.
- Novices, children and the desperate move their lips or whisper. **Lip-reading the prayer** is how true names leak; noble children are drilled never to move their lips.
- **Three tiers of invocation** (example wearer):
  - True name, spoken: strongest (e.g. **blue**)
  - True name, thought: one step down (e.g. **cyan**)
  - **Epithet** ("the Grey Lady"): much weaker (e.g. **yellow**), and the ancestor is **slower to respond**
- Families show the epithet in public and keep the true name secret. Public displays of colour may therefore *understate* a family's real strength, which complicates the colour-as-reputation economy (see §4.2).
- Narrative device: the first time the impostor must Mantle in public, she *must not* move her lips, because the household's true name is at stake.

**[Canon] Revive aura blast**
- Not an explosion; a **gale-like push** that can knock bystanders off their feet.
- **Proportional to the wounds being healed.** It is the ancestors warding off attackers during the vulnerable moment of revival, buying the wearer time.
- [Proposal] Implications: a big blast tells everyone nearby how badly the wearer was hurt; seasoned duellists read the gust as information. Healing indoors wrecks rooms. A small gust after a "fatal" blow can reveal a faked death.

**[Proposal] Craft Mantle rule-bends**
- Only **Myth-touched ancestors** carry a small, legend-derived rule-bend (e.g. a "Frankenstein" Mantle for bio-punk surgery). Rare, specific, story-rooted.
- "Myth-touched" means the ancestor was shaped by Myth **in life** (a person who partly gave in to whispers but died human), or **after death** through legend distorting how they are remembered. *Not* the same as "Myth-born" creatures or their descendants.
- **Anyone** who wears the Mantle can access the rule-bend; it belongs to the Mantle, not the bloodline.
- Corollary: Myth-touched Mantles are a grey area. The Inquisition may tolerate, regulate or ban them, since using one is a small brush with Myth.

### 1.2 The Approved Canon (Sanctioned Magic)

[Proposal] The legal list. Hedge-witch magic is deliberately left open.

**Why sanctioned magic is safe:** under the Myth rules, **widespread belief lets a drop of Myth fuel a reliable effect**. The Concord *wants* these spells common, standardised and boring, because familiarity keeps them stable. Every entry is taught from an approved text, under the consensus Role (see §1.3 "madhouse").

**Licensing tiers**
- **Household:** anyone may use it. Sold by licensed apothecaries and chandlers or taught in catechism.
- **Licensed:** requires a guild or Office licence (physicians, notaries, assayers).
- **Restricted / Crown:** military. The Concord dislikes it but the Crown won't budge.

### 1.3 Myth

**[Canon]**
- Knowledge of Myth is tightly controlled. Public misunderstanding could change its fundamental nature.
- The Inquisition once crusaded against the "Fallen" (mystics). The crusade's own expectation ("anyone who taps in will fall") caused Myths to erupt.
- Through confessions they gained insider understanding, then reluctantly suppressed the topic to let Myth "reset" over a generation or two: slow, lightly tappable, lower risk.
- They now aim to control **Myth as a whole**, not just individual Mystics.
- Formal magic study is its own sanctioned **Role** that channels Myth in known, socially accepted ways. Nobles encourage it to complement Mantles.
- Most youths don't recognise formal study as Myth. Only those who follow heathen whispers (by accident or on purpose) are at serious risk.
- Early Myth flowed **from the in-group toward pariahs** (hedge witches, travellers, foreigners, individuals) and **toward focal points** (leaders, wise folk, physical deities). Only with travel, population and clashing opinions did Myth rival Mantles in power.

**[Proposal]**
**[Canon]**
- **The Inquisition is itself a small, intensely believing group**, so inquisitors risk their own Myths: the Hound, the Witchfinder, "one who can smell the Fallen."
- **Formal magic is a madhouse.** Students read the ramblings of past scholars, are baffled for weeks, then suddenly get results once the Student/Scholar Role takes hold. Each scholar credits whatever ritual they happened to be doing at the time (like Skinner's "superstitious pigeons"). Rival scholars insisting the other is "doing magic wrong" is comic material. Over time a **generally accepted consensus** forms, regardless of each person's path to it.
- **Myth and consent stay deliberately nebulous.** How much the subconscious can pull on Myth without active choice is left undefined.
- **The twins are closer to Myth-born** (shaped by the Lord's "unified family" image projected outward) than directly warped by in-house belief.
- **The eldest daughter's experience stays ambiguous.** She is never a POV character, though her death drives much of the narrative.

**[Proposal]**
- **Storybooks and fairy tales** are uncontrollable Myth leaks. MC being a fictive from such a book fits.
- [Proposal] Madhouse corollaries:
  - Textbooks are full of contradictory marginalia; second-hand grimoires are prized for their annotations.
  - Schools of magic are really schools of *superstition*: lineages of teachers passing on their own rituals.
  - The consensus *is* the Role. Heretical scholars who reject it see their spells weaken. That's a very quiet way for the Inquisition to enforce orthodoxy: control the consensus textbook.

**[Open]**- Who passes down the true understanding of Myth, and how? Proposal: Inquisition archives, private oral traditions in old families (revealed in degrees, Freemason-style, each degree its own Role), and a rival underground canon among the hags.
- What is the public's official explanation for the monsters left over from the crusade?

## 2. Deep History (From First Settlements Onward)

**[Canon]** Ancestors and Myth have existed since ~12,000 years before the story. Great men *and* women are remembered, worshipped and Mantled. Polytheism.

**[Proposal] Dominoes**
1. **Memory is military technology.** Genealogists, bards and lineage-keepers are as vital as smiths. Writing may arise to preserve names and fighting styles rather than grain tallies.
2. **Conquest = erasing ancestors.** Shrines burned, names chiselled away (*damnatio memoriae*), cult statues carried off. Defeated peoples' heroes can be **adopted by the victors** (cultural appropriation as literal power). Ancestors might hold grudges.
3. **Women in power from the start.** Bilateral inheritance is likely; a **bride brings her ancestors** in her dowry; marriage merges ancestor cults. Mantle-assisted childbirth dramatically raises noblewomen's survival. Patriarchy is one possible path, not a given.
4. **Champion warfare dominates for millennia.** Pre-battle duels are decisive. Commoner armies matter for pinning, sieges and occupation.
5. **Architecture and etiquette shaped by the strain limit.** Wide noble grounds, Mantle districts, fortresses designed around 100 m. Doff at the door; only the sovereign may be Mantled in the throne room; one Mantle per ballroom.
6. **Entrenched elites.** Revives give old champions long careers. Assassination targets the hours after someone doffs (baths, bedchambers, poison).
7. **Pariah Myth.** Every village makes its own monsters by exile (scapegoat / *pharmakos*). **The wild is a fossil record of old prejudice.** Myth-born beings that stabilise into species show what each culture once feared.
8. **Guest-right is protective magic.** Bread, a name and a hearth pull a stranger into the in-group and halt pariah-Myth. Breaking hospitality is the gravest sin.
9. **Fae as fallen traders.** Perpetual outsiders become bargain-bound, unable to lie, unable to enter uninvited. Fae law is a merchant survival code made nature.
10. **Sacred kings.** Kings worshipped too long turn into Myth-monsters; kings who die in time become clean ancestors and Mantles. Ritual king-killing or abdication after a set term (Frazer's *Golden Bough*). **This Mantle-vs-Myth tension can be the engine of political history.**
11. **Myth grows with communication:**

| Era | Effect |
|---|---|
| Cities | Local cults strengthen; first recorded falls |
| Empires & roads | Shared beliefs → larger Myths; conquered monsters spread |
| Printing | **The Inquisition's crusade** (our witch-hunt peak 1560–1630 followed printing and the *Malleus Maleficarum*) |
| 19th century | Newspapers, mass literacy, penny dreadfuls, railways: **the most dangerous century yet** |

12. **Mantled farmers [Canon: farmers Mantle nearly all the time]** → higher food output → bigger populations → Myth grows earlier. Less need for large-scale slave labour. Peasant revolts are far more dangerous (8× pressure plus a scythe), so lords push Mantle-restriction laws.
13. **Industrialisation:** the strain limit keeps Mantled industry small and dispersed. **Machines don't count toward the limit**, so factories win. Hence a Luddite-style conflict between Mantled craftsmen and machines, fused with ancestor worship.

**Real-world references:** Alonso de Salazar Frías (Basque witch trials, 1609–14); the Benandanti (Ginzburg, *The Night Battles*); the Index of Forbidden Books; Catholic *latria / hyperdulia / dulia*; *interpretatio romana*; the Papacy outliving Rome.

## 3. Religion & the Inquisition

**[Canon]**
- Polytheistic world; the line between ancestor and god is thin.
- The Inquisition is the powerful remnant of an empire that elevated **one god** (or a select few, like Game of Thrones' Faith of the Seven) above the other ancestors, who are ranked as lesser demigods in **tiers of veneration**.
- Gods are capped like any Mantle. Grounded: a violet still falls to a trained army.
- The high god's Mantle is eldritch, **warped by the Myth of both believers and detractors**.

**[Proposal]**
- Tiered veneration modelled on *latria / hyperdulia / dulia*.
- **Canonization = regulation.** The Church decides which ancestors may be venerated. Unsanctioned ancestor cults are heresy and fertile ground for Myth.
- **Assimilation by renaming** (*interpretatio*) rewrites conquered ancestors through Myth.
- **The god's Mantle is internally contradictory.** Doctrine changes its moveset; schisms fork it; mastery is impossible because its "lifestyle" is a contradiction. Clergy need it but never own it, and wearing it pushes contradictory impulses into them, so high clergy become **uncanny**.
- **Core role of the Inquisition in the 19th century:** censorship of the press, gossip columns and fairy tales. Its enemy is the printing press more than individual Mystics.
- The god may be the largest Myth-being in existence, which would explain the Inquisition's expertise and fear.

## 3b. The "Inquisition": Name & Role in Society

### [Proposal] Naming
Real precedents: the Roman Inquisition was renamed the **Holy Office**, then the **Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith**. Its sister body, the **Congregatio de Propaganda Fide** (1622), is where the word *propaganda* comes from. Their real job is controlling belief, memory and narrative, so their name should sound like *harmony* rather than *hunting*.

- **Official name:** the **Holy Office of Concord** (*concord* = agreement of hearts, i.e. consensus). Members are **Concordants**.
- **Public / administrative nickname:** **the Censors** (from the Roman office that kept the census and policed morals: registers, licences, reputations).
- **Whispered nickname:** **the Quiet**, because their work is silence and erasure. Saying their full name casually is unlucky (folk Myth hygiene).
- Field agents who hunt Myth-touched: **Hounds** (folk) or **Wardens** (official).
- Their registry of tolerated Myth-touched: **the Ledger** (hence "Ledgered").
- Remnant of an empire, so transnational like the Papacy: an envoy at every court (**legate**), and kings need its blessing.

### [Proposal] Role in the Season & politics
1. **Licensing the narrative.** The Office licenses plays, novels, newspapers and **society columns** (compare the British Lord Chamberlain, who licensed plays from 1737 to 1968). Gossip columnists work under its permission. The Office reads everything.
2. **Rivals of the hostesses.** Society patronesses and the Office both control belief. Uneasy alliance, quiet war. **The Lady's hag network is the Office's natural enemy.**
3. **Presentation at court** requires a certificate of good standing (catechism, confirmation) and a blessing from a Concordant chaplain. **A debutante is inspected before she's presented.**
4. **Heraldry of ancestors.** A College of Arms equivalent registers each house's **public epithets** and approved ancestors. Debuts and marriages trigger a review of the family's registered ancestors. A house can be "unregistered," which is a social death.
5. **Marriage.** The Office issues **banns** (public announcements inviting objections, which in this world also invite belief) and **dispensations**. Marriage merges ancestor cults, so it needs Office approval. **A possible reason the contract is existential:** a match with a house in good standing launders "storied blood" and keeps the Office off the family's back.
6. **Household chaplains and confessors.** Noble houses keep an Office-appointed chaplain, effectively a resident spy. **How does the manor handle theirs?** Bribed, Myth-touched, blackmailed, dead, or dangerously absent?
7. **Hounds at balls.** Uncanny Concordants attend major events. Their presence alone is pressure: a debutante with secrets must stay calm before someone who might "smell" her.
8. **Careers for the unplaced.** Younger sons and unmarriageable daughters go into the Office (as historically into the Church and convents). It's a threat ("behave or the cloister"), a refuge, and a way to plant family members inside it.
9. **Factions.** **Purifiers** (crusade nostalgists, open hunts) vs **Quietists** (the current doctrine: suppress, let Myth fade). The Quietist approach is fragile, and one public scandal could hand the Purifiers power. **A Myth scandal in the Season could restart a crusade**, giving the plot high stakes.

## 3c. Reach of the Faith & the Concord

### [Canon]
- **The Lord's border march is a conquered province where the high god is still feared.**
- The high god's Mantle **cannot practically be wielded**, so people drift away from it toward practical ancestors and gods. It is still spoken of in reverent whispers.

### Historical reference: how far did the Inquisition reach?
| Institution | Dates | Reach |
|---|---|---|
| **Medieval (papal) Inquisition** | 1184 (episcopal), 1231 (papal, run by Dominicans and Franciscans) to the 1400s | Southern France (the Cathars), Italy, Aragon, sporadically Germany and Bohemia. **England never had one;** bishops and the Crown handled heresy themselves. |
| **Spanish Inquisition** | 1478–1834 | Spain, Sicily, Sardinia, the Canaries, and the Americas (Lima 1570, Mexico 1571, Cartagena 1610), reaching the Philippines through Mexico. **Controlled by the Crown, not the Pope.** Roughly 150,000 tried, and an estimated 3,000–5,000 executed. |
| **Portuguese Inquisition** | 1536–1821 | Portugal, Brazil (through visitations), and **Goa** (1560–1812). |
| **Roman Inquisition (Holy Office)** | 1542 onward | The Italian states. **Venice limited it** by requiring lay assessors, and Naples refused the Spanish model. It still exists as the Dicastery for the Doctrine of the Faith. |
| **Not present** | — | The Protestant north after the 1520s, and France in the early modern period, where the parlements handled heresy. |

**Lesson:**
- **The faith was wide but shallow; the tribunals were narrow but deep.**
- Catholicism covered western Europe, but the Inquisition existed **only where a crown found it useful** and allowed it in.
- It was strongest as a *state tool* (Spain, Portugal) and weakest where local powers guarded their own jurisdiction (Venice, England).

### [Proposal] In-world: a *deus otiosus*
- In anthropology, a ***deus otiosus*** ("idle god") is a high creator god who has withdrawn. People acknowledge it but rarely petition it; daily worship goes to closer, more useful powers. Examples are **Olorun** (Yoruba) and **Ouranos.**
- **The high god fits exactly:**
  - Everyone honours it in name.
  - Almost no one prays to it for help, because its Mantle can't be worn.
  - Daily life runs on ancestors and practical gods.
- **Why the Concord attaches itself to an unwieldable god:**
  - **No one can embody it, so no one can challenge the Concord's authority to speak for it.**
  - An empty throne is the safest one to serve.
  - The Concord's *actual* value to every crown is **managing Myth**, not the god itself.
- **The Saint upsets this.** A living person who bears the god's Mantle threatens to turn an idle god into an active one. That means:
  - a **revival** (compare the Great Awakenings),
  - renewed fear in conquered lands,
  - and a Concord with a live weapon instead of an empty throne.

**Geography of belief:**
| Zone | The god | The Concord |
|---|---|---|
| **Heartland** (the old conquering tribe's homeland, the capital) | Revered and formal; state ceremony | Strongest. Allied with the Crown. Tribunals active. |
| **Converted provinces** | Veneer over ancestor cults. **Syncretism:** local gods are recast as the high god's saints or servants (compare Brigid the goddess becoming St Brigid). | Present, but negotiating with local lords |
| **Conquered borderlands** (the march) | **Feared.** Remembered as a conqueror; folk stories of eyes and wings. | Watchful, unpopular, relies on the Crown's garrison |
| **Foreign realms** | Other pantheons; the god is seen as a foreign war god | No tribunals. Influence through diplomacy, scholarship and shared Myth-management (a "Concord of nations") |

### [Canon] Abroad
- The Concord keeps eyes on **military fronts and newly occupied lands**.
- Because of the training and secrecy involved, it relies on a **less effective outer circle** for distant business. Members of the outer circle are not fully in on the secret.
- **Its influence in other kingdoms is limited.**
- **The Concord is distinct from the Crown.** Its territory doesn't follow the kingdom's borders: it holds **holy sites of power along an old, storied pilgrimage route**, and those sites now fall on various sides of the frontiers.

### Historical reference: Church territory across borders
- **The Papal States** were a sovereign strip across central Italy. **Avignon** was a papal enclave inside France until 1791.
- **The Prince-Bishoprics** of the Holy Roman Empire: Mainz, Cologne and Trier were bishops who ruled as princes and voted for the Emperor.
- **The military orders:**
  - The Templars and Hospitallers held commanderies across every kingdom.
  - The Hospitallers ruled Rhodes, then Malta, and still exist as a sovereign entity without territory.
- **Mount Athos** is a self-governing monastic republic inside Greece to this day.
- **Pilgrimage routes** crossed borders under special protection: the **Camino de Santiago** (France → Spain) and the **Via Francigena** (Canterbury → Rome). Pilgrims carried safe-conduct letters.
- **Familiars of the Spanish Inquisition:** lay informants and agents, often numbering in the thousands. They received privileges such as the right to bear arms and immunity from royal courts. They are a direct model for the outer circle.
- **Building over older sites:** churches were often built on pagan holy places (the Pantheon, and the policy in Pope Gregory's letter to Mellitus in 601).

### [Proposal] The Way and its Stations
- **The route:** an old pilgrimage path following the conquering tribe's original campaign, or a founding saint's journey. Each stop is a **Station**, matching the earlier "stations" vocabulary.
- **Status of the Stations:**
  - They are **Concord enclaves under their own jurisdiction**, much like embassies.
  - Each offers **sanctuary** and has a small garrison of battle-sisters.
  - The core Stations in the heartland are run by the inner circle; distant ones by the outer circle.
- **Pilgrimage feeds Myth:**
  - Pilgrims carry stories along the route.
  - The Way is the Concord's oldest **belief pipeline**, and the reason the god's Mantle is most stable at the Stations.
  - **The Saint's tour along the Way** is both PR and maintenance of her Myth.
- **Neutral ground in wartime:**
  - Stations on foreign soil serve for talks, prisoner exchanges and ransoms.
  - They are also havens for spies and for smuggling.
  - Both crowns resent them.
- **[Proposal] Outer-circle name: "Familiars"** (or "Tertiaries," after lay Third Orders).
  - They know the rules but not the reasons, so they enforce them mechanically.
  - **An overzealous Familiar abroad can trigger exactly the kind of Myth panic the Concord exists to prevent.**
- **The Station in the march:**
  - It sits on **a local ancestor's shrine, built over during the conquest.**
  - The locals still remember whose holy place it really is.

## 4. Society & Mantle Etiquette

### 4.1 Detection & signalling
**[Canon]** Pressure is felt within 100 m; the gradient is perceptible every 10–15 m; walls don't block it.

**[Proposal]**
- **Mantling is drawing a sword.** It's public and can't be hidden. Tactical question: who commits first.
- Sentries **triangulate** Mantles by comparing pressure; trained servants and guards keep "Mantle-watch."
- **Commoners doff when a lord passes**, like removing a hat. Refusing is an insult.
- A host's Mantle at a ball means "this house is guarded." A guest Mantling is a scandal.
- **Children raised near a Mantle feel the household's mood through their skin.** In the manor, the Lord's Mantle is the weather.
- No stone shelter is possible, so secret rooms need *distance*, not walls. Manors with deep grounds or cellar wings far from the main hall are prized.

### 4.2 Colour as private information
**[Canon]** Colour is visible but only known by reputation or witnessed fights.

**[Proposal]**
- Families guard colours like finances. Every duel *reveals* information. Bluffing and "paper violets" exist.
- Colour belongs to the **wearer**, not the ancestor. Violet mother, orange heir: an embarrassment that can't be hidden forever.
- **Betrothal demonstrations** of a bride's colour, as dowries were inspected.

### 4.3 Who gets the slot (class & profession)
**[Proposal]**

| Class | Attitude |
|---|---|
| Old nobility | *Sprezzatura*. Mantles for war, duels and dynastic rites only. Mantling for social skills is vulgar ("brandishing a sword in a salon"). |
| New money | Uses craft Mantles openly; mocked as **"borrowed hands."** |
| Guilds / middle class | The Mantle is certification: apprentice with it, journeyman, then master without it. Never doffing = perpetual journeyman. |
| Labourers | Purely practical. The foreman (or family) assigns the shift's Mantle. Being "the field's Mantle" is a status role. |

- **Medicine:** patients may need the slot more than surgeons (revives during an operation). The rich wear their own Mantle and hire an unmantled star surgeon; the poor get a Mantled apprentice and no safety net. The same applies in childbirth.
- **Performance:** a declared Mantled concert is a séance ("hear the composer's own interpretation"). An undeclared one is fraud. Norm: **always declare your Mantle.**
- **Over-reliance insults:** "hollow," "puppet," "worn." [Open] Is there a noble equivalent for those who can't fight unmantled?
- **Lifestyle requirement makes nobles conservative.** Heirs must live like their founders: hunting, duelling, warrior codes, archaic dress. Compare the samurai keeping their code.
- **Commoners can beat nobles** (level playing field). Nobles' real edge is training, secret ancestors and rare movesets. **Commoners' ancestors are public; nobles' are secret.** That is the real class line.

### 4.4 Craft Mantle incentives
**[Proposal]**
1. **Pressure is force**: 8× at 10 cm for smiths, masons, tanners.
2. **Toughness offsets occupational hazards**: glassblowers, miners, gilders (mercury), alchemists. Low status for the elite, survival for workers.
3. **Myth-touched ancestors carry a small legend-derived rule-bend** (e.g. a Frankenstein Mantle), usable by any wearer (see §1.1).
4. **Legend becomes moveset quirks**: "never retreated" → no backstep.

### 4.5 Same Mantle, many wearers
**[Canon]** Allowed; no strength scaling.

**[Proposal]**
- **Fighting-game metagame:** top-tier Mantles have public frame data and drilled counters. **Rare Mantles are valuable for surprise.**
- **True names are secrets.** Families invoke the founder's true name privately and an epithet publicly. A betrayed name means a stolen Mantle. Marriage may involve sharing the name. Echoes fae true-name lore.
- **Canon consolidation:** popular ancestors get remembered, obscure ones fade like dead languages. Hermits push back.
- **The hermit's dilemma:** a new Mantle only survives if remembered, so the secret master must eventually become famous.

### 4.6 Cheap death & violence
**[Canon]** Ultra-violence and retribution are normalised among the Mantled. Duels to first death. Hidden corporal punishment. Exhausted soldiers are captured safely, but everything before that is barbaric.

**[Proposal]**
- **Revives are a resource; the cost is downtime.** Duelling flaunts wealth: spare revives, guards, safe rooms.
- **Violence is cheap only for the Mantled.** Bystanders suffer revive blasts and street duels. Two legal regimes: Mantled-on-Mantled vs everyone else.
- **Forensics fail** because healing erases evidence. Courts rely on testimony and reputation, so **trials are belief contests** that feed Myth.
- **Executions of nobles** are long spectacles (kill three times, or hold them until exhausted).
- **Capture and ransom (parole) culture** around exhausted soldiers.
- **The Inquisition extracted its insider knowledge through kill-revive interrogation.**

### 4.7 Pregnancy & confinement
**[Canon]** Mantle healing protects the mother; a Mantle held for weeks before birth strengthens the baby but is fragile against any second Mantle.

**[Proposal]**
- Historical **lying-in confinement** gains a mechanical reason: a **Mantle quarantine** of the birthing wing.
- **Confinement is the household's weakest window** for raids, coups and assassinations.
- **Murder by walking in Mantled:** deniable sabotage that forces the mother to doff. Feeds the Lady's paranoia about her stillbirths.
- Children "born under" the confinement Mantle; astrology-like traditions; families fight over which ancestor.
- With the Lady nearly always pregnant, **the Lord could rarely Mantle at home**, which pushes him toward Myth.
- **The twins were born nearly the same day:** two confinements, one Mantle slot. Who got it, who was moved, and was the other child weaker? A source of resentment, linked to the twin killing.
- Regeneration interaction: the confinement Mantle must belong to an ancestor who understood childbirth (see §1.1, "witnessed wounds").

## 4b. Marriage Economics: Dowry vs Bride Price

### Reference
| | **Dowry** | **Bride price (bridewealth)** |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Bride's family → couple/groom | Groom's family → bride's family |
| Logic | Daughter's share of inheritance, paid early | Compensation for losing her labour and fertility; buys the children into the groom's lineage |
| Typical societies | Plough agriculture, landed, stratified, monogamous Eurasia (Goody, 1973) | Hoe farming and herding, where women's labour is central (much of Africa, parts of Asia) |
| Daughters are | A cost; families compete to marry them **up** (hypergamy) | An asset |
| Side effects | Dowry inflation (Florence created a public dowry fund, the *Monte delle doti*; Venice capped dowries by law); surplus daughters sent to convents | Polygyny for rich men; young poor men can't marry (tension, raiding); a sister's bridewealth pays for her brother's wife; divorce means repaying, so families pressure wives to stay |
| Woman's leverage | Dowry often legally hers or returned at widowhood (Roman *dos*) | Little personal property, but strong backing from her own kin |

A third form: **dower / morning gift / mahr**, paid by the groom **to the bride herself** as personal security.

**19th-century British aristocracy:** the bride's **portion** (dowry) was exchanged for **jointure** (her income as a widow) and **pin money** (her personal allowance), all in lawyer-drafted settlements.

### [Proposal] For this world: a mixed, colour-priced market
- Baseline is **dowry** (the default for a landed, stratified society), but the dowry includes **ancestors**: rights to rites, shrines and possibly **shared true names** (a "dowry of names").
- **Strong daughters flip the flow.** A woman with a high colour brings military value and Mantle-protected births of strong heirs, so for her the **groom's family pays** (bride-price-like). **The marriage market is priced by colour.** A yellow child with a promising future is a good investment, and a light-green adult is a disappointment that can lower the price.
- **Mantle-holding brides as dower:** a bride may bring a personal Mantle/ancestor the husband's house can't touch, as her own security (like a morning gift).
- **Hypergamy pressure** plus Office approval of ancestors creates convent pressure (the Office's sisterhood takes surplus daughters).

### [Proposal] Refined rule: the balancing payment
- Payment flows **toward the more valuable party**, regardless of gender. It's a balancing payment: whoever is "marrying up" pays the difference.
- **Second variable: who leaves their house.** The house that loses a member (and their ancestors' rites) to the other is compensated. A strong son marrying *into* his wife's house brings his family a payment.
- Dowry is the default only because brides usually join the husband's house and evenly matched brides "marry up" by custom.

### [Proposal] Eldest daughter's contract timeline
- In real canon law, a **betrothal (*sponsalia de futuro*) was valid from age 7** and revocable at puberty; marriage was allowed at 12 for girls and 14 for boys. This world's contracts would add **heavy penalty clauses** so a prodigy's betrothal can't be walked back.
- **Age 5–6 (the war):** while the Lord is away, the Lady pushes Mantle training and Myth, parades the girl through her network, and **negotiates the match herself**.
- **Age 7 (the Lord's victorious return, his year 10):** the house's prestige peaks and she shows **yellow** publicly. The betrothal is formalised.
- **Bride price in instalments:** a large first payment at betrothal (an exclusivity fee to beat rival suitors), the rest at the wedding. The first payment covers **war debts** (nobles largely paid for their own troops).
- **Why hide her death (age 12)?**
  - Death normally voids a betrothal, but the money must be returned, and it's spent.
  - **Suicide carries legal and religious ruin.** In England, suicide (*felo de se*) meant forfeiting goods to the Crown (until 1870) and being refused normal church burial (until 1823). Here, a prodigy's suicide also invites an **Office inquest**, which could uncover the heretical Myth behind her talent.
  - Hiding the death avoids repayment, ruin and the inquest all at once.

### [Canon] She marries out
**For the Lord:**
- Secrets stay home; the outsider never lives inside the manor.
- He collects a large bride price and an alliance with a higher house.
- He keeps his seat and power; a Mantle-and-Myth lord wouldn't willingly retire.
- The daughter becomes his eyes inside a stronger house.
- The 1st son (a year younger) is a perfectly adequate heir; a prodigy is worth more as an investment than as an heir.

**For the story:**
- **The impostor lives inside a hostile, stronger house**, under scrutiny of her body, colour, fertility (heirs expected) and the Office's attention at a higher court.
- **This is the villainess trope made existential.** If the fiancé breaks the engagement (say, for a heroine), the bride price must be repaid, which means ruin and an inquest. The villainess can't simply step aside gracefully.
- **Brothers wearing each other's inheritance:**
  - The 1st son was the heir and gave it up to become the "daughter."
  - From age 15 to 19, the real 2nd son was the heir on paper while also being groomed as the backup "daughter."
  - At the swap, the 1st son takes over the 2nd son's identity and, with it, the heirship.
  - Each brother ends up living the other's life, which is strong fuel for their rivalry.

**Notes correction:** the original notes say the 1st son was raised as "2nd heir in case something happened to the daughter." Under this model he was **the heir**, and she was **the house's investment**. That makes his choice at age 15 heavier: he gave up the inheritance to take her place.

(Rejected for now: heiress plus a groom who marries in, the dower-house retirement, a complicit groom. They cost the Lord power and drain tension.)

### [Proposal] Story hook: why the contract is existential
The eldest daughter's contract was signed **as a bride-price deal** because she was a promising young yellow. **The other house has already paid in advance** (money, military backing, a loan, or Office influence), and the manor **has already spent or used it**, perhaps during the war. If no bride is delivered, the price must be repaid, which they can't do, and the breach of contract invites scrutiny. The impostor isn't just saving face; she is **collateral for a debt**.

## 4d. Love, Sex & Lineage: Same-Sex & Plural Relationships

### Historical reference (c. 1780–1914)
- **Men:**
  - Sodomy was a capital crime in England until **1861**.
  - "Gross indecency" was criminalized in **1885** (the Labouchere Amendment), and **Wilde** was convicted under it in **1895**.
  - Aristocrats were mostly protected by discretion and money, until a scandal broke.
- **Women:** sex between women was **never criminalized** in Britain. Society mostly refused to imagine it, which gave women cover.
  - **Romantic friendship** was socially accepted. Love letters, shared beds and lifelong devotion between women were not considered suspect.
  - **The Ladies of Llangollen** (1780 onward) were two Irish aristocrats who eloped and lived together for 50 years. They were visited by Wellington and Wordsworth.
  - **Anne Lister** was a Yorkshire landowner whose coded diaries are explicit. In 1834 she and Ann Walker took communion together as a private marriage.
  - **"Boston marriages"** were two women sharing a household for life.
- **Polyamory and arrangements:**
  - **The Devonshire ménage** (already cited): Georgiana raised Bess Foster's children by the Duke.
  - **The "heir and a spare" rule:** once a wife had produced heirs, discreet affairs on both sides were tolerated among the elite.
  - **Edward VII and Alice Keppel:** a royal mistress was openly accommodated.
  - **Vita Sackville-West and Harold Nicolson** (married 1913): both were queer, each had same-sex affairs, and they had a devoted, lifelong marriage. **This is the closest model for the impostor and her betrothed.**
- **Gender variance:**
  - **The Chevalier d'Éon** lived the second half of their life as a woman; their sex was publicly debated and wagered on.
  - **James Barry** was a British army surgeon who lived as a man.
  - **Consummation and proof:**
    - Non-consummation was grounds for annulment.
    - France held **"impotence trials,"** including *congrès* with witnesses (16th–17th c.).
    - **The wedding night was a legal event.**

### [Proposal] In-world logic: the sin is a barren line, not the act
- In an ancestor-centred society, **duty is to the line**: produce heirs, keep the ancestors fed with descendants.
- Same-sex love isn't the core transgression. **Failing to continue the line is.**
- **The orthodox rule (spoken quietly): "Duty first, then discretion."**
  - Once the heir and spare exist, what you do in private is your own business, as long as it stays private.
- **Myth makes discretion literal, not just polite.** Gossip about a liaison turns it into a story, and stories have weight. Scandal is a Myth hazard as well as a social one, which is why the elite keep queer arrangements so quiet.
- **Adoption** (Roman and Japanese style) can carry a line. Childless couples, same-sex couples among them, adopt nephews or worthy juniors and Mantle them under the house ancestors.
- **Weapon-women:**
  - Battle-sister bonds are a recognized, romanticized form of romantic friendship.
  - Compare the Sacred Band of Thebes: paired lovers who fought side by side.
  - Two weapon-women setting up house after service is a known, gently mocked type.
- **The Concord:**
  - Officially disapproving.
  - It is more concerned with **Myth from scandal** than with the act itself.
  - It mostly enforces against the *public*. Private life is left alone.

### [Canon] The impostor and her betrothed
- **The impostor transitioned physically to a woman and is still attracted to women.** This is a very common experience among trans women.
- **The discovery scene:**
  - The betrothed walks in on her in a bad state, or uncovers the secret some other way.
  - The host panics and tries to justify himself, which only confirms the betrothed's fears and prejudices.
  - **The Tulpa fronts to salvage it:**
    - She promises to be inspected on the wedding night.
    - She asks for patience during this "in-between" state.
    - She offers to accept a mistress and raise the mistress's children as her own.
    - She asks him to match her efforts toward the alliance.
- **Later rapport:**
  - The impostor is as taken with the heroine (the new Saint) as any male lead.
  - She notices her betrothed's infatuation and encourages it, at her own expense.
  - When he picks up on her feelings, they share a quiet moment of mutual understanding.

### [Proposal] Notes on the scene and the arrangement
- **The Tulpa's speech is historically sound.** The wedding night was a legal test of the marriage, and offering to accept a mistress and raise her children is exactly Georgiana's arrangement. It reads as aristocratic, political and painfully generous.
- **The inspection gives him a pass/fail test** instead of an open-ended fear. Pressure-tested men respond well to that.
- **The power shift:**
  - He now holds her secret, and it is **leverage.**
  - Her offer matters because it gives him something in exchange: **a guaranteed heir line through a mistress, if he needs one.**
- **Myth twist: his belief matters.** If the transition is Myth-fed, the people closest to her and their beliefs feed it.
  - **The betrothed accepting her as a woman literally helps her become one;** his doubt could slow or warp it.
  - This makes the romance subplot mechanically important as well as emotionally important.
- **Toward a Vita–Harold marriage:**
  - Two people who both love women, bound by duty, grow into allies and real affection.
  - Shared longing for the Saint becomes the bond.
- **Polyamory paths:**
  1. **Shared yearning, nothing acted on.** It's bittersweet and the Saint stays out of reach.
  2. **The betrothed courts or keeps the Saint openly,** while the impostor has a discreet romantic friendship with her.
  3. **A full triad** under the cover of companion and lady-in-waiting. A lady with a beloved companion is completely respectable, as with the Ladies of Llangollen.
- **The Saint is the biggest complication:**
  - She is the Concord's living icon. Any romance with her is a Concord matter.
  - Gossip about the Saint's love life risks twisting her Myth, as happens to anyone the public obsesses over.

### [Canon] Fertility by belief
- **The impostor can be fertile by the wedding, if enough people believe her to be.**

- **The betrothed believing he's marrying a "man," and gossiping about it to his confidants, is an existential threat** to the whole plan. That is the reason for the host's initial panic.

### [Proposal] The Saint's road to a triad
**Precedents:**
- **The Vestal Virgins:** priestesses whose chastity guaranteed Rome's safety. If found unchaste, they were buried alive.
- **Joan of Arc:** her virginity ("La Pucelle") was central to her legitimacy and was physically examined.
- **Married saints:** **Elizabeth of Hungary** and **Bridget of Sweden** were wives and mothers whose holiness came from charity and devotion, not virginity.

**The core problem:**
- If the Concord built her sainthood partly from public belief, which is **sanctioned, managed Myth**, then **her purity is load-bearing.**
- A scandal about her love life could do more than ruin her reputation. It could **weaken or warp her healing**, turning the Saint into the "Harlot Saint."
- **Love literally threatens her power.**

**Internal struggles:**
- **Faith versus truth:**
  - She sincerely believes.
  - To join the triad she must learn that the impostor is Myth-touched, which her faith calls Fallen.
  - She may also discover that **the Concord manufactures saints from the same forbidden force it hunts.** That is a crisis of faith, not just of love.
- **A self made by others:**
  - She has been a ward and a symbol, with no identity outside the Role.
  - **She mirrors the impostor:** both are public continuities shaped by other people's belief. This could be the deepest point of rapport between them.
- **Orthodox guilt:**
  - She desires a woman.
  - She desires a betrothed man, which makes her "the other woman" in the classic otome-villainess frame.
- **Class and agency:**
  - For a low-born girl, the only place beside a nobleman is **mistress**, a step down from sainthood.
  - She has always been owned by the Concord. Joining a house risks just trading one owner for another. **She has to choose it, not be acquired.**
- **Duty to report:** loving the impostor means hiding heresy from her own handlers.

**External struggles:**
- **The Concord:**
  - She is its most valuable propaganda asset. Her confessor and handlers watch her, and Hounds attend her appearances.
  - They may plan to keep her celibate, or to marry her strategically to a royal or an ally.
  - **Has she taken final vows?** If not, she has a narrow window.
- **The public Myth:** gossip linking her to the couple could curdle her Myth. Three stories must hold at once:
  - the impostor is a woman (and fertile),
  - the Saint is holy,
  - the marriage is ordinary.
- **The Lord's household:**
  - A healer whose senses are trained on wounds could notice the anemia, the feeding and the Myth.
  - **Keeping her close endangers the family secret.** This is one reason the impostor marrying *out* is a blessing.
- **Rivals:** the other male leads, her Concord-chosen match, and rival debutantes who would destroy her with a whisper.
- **Legal:**
  - She cannot marry the betrothed.
  - Children she bore him would be bastards, **unless the Tulpa's promise pays off.**

**Possible routes to the ending:**
1. **The promise comes true.** The Tulpa's words, "I will welcome her children as my own," spoken in desperation, become the triad's foundation. The dramatic irony pays off.
2. **The household chaplaincy as cover:**
   - Noble houses can ask for a Concord sister as household healer or chaplain.
   - The Saint living in the impostor's *new* home, away from the Lord, is completely respectable.
   - The Concord might even approve, wanting eyes inside a powerful house.
3. **Reshaping her Myth:** she moves from "virgin Saint" to **"Saint of the Hearth,"** holy through charity and devotion like Elizabeth of Hungary.
   - This needs a **deliberate public campaign**, which echoes the impostor's own Role-craft. The impostor teaches the Saint how to steer a Myth.
   - A Concord faction might back it.
4. **Leaving with honour:** a public, dignified withdrawal from active sainthood, for example after an act of great service. That frees her, but costs the Concord its icon, so they resist.

### [Canon] The Saint's power: Mantle *and* Myth
- **Origin:**
  - She showed devotion early enough to invoke **the orthodox god's eldritch, paradoxical Mantle.**
  - She had enough skill at **hiding its side effects** that people came to believe she could wield it properly, as few can.
- **The Concord's investigation:**
  - Afraid the side effects would show, she **subconsciously leaned into her Myth**, and the side effects gradually vanished.
  - She believes she hid it from the Concord's men. **They saw it, and they understood what it meant.**
  - They know she is Myth-touched, and they took her in to **steer it themselves.**
- **Her progression:**
  1. At first her most visible role is as a **stable vessel** for the god's Mantle and benevolence.
  2. As the Concord's PR spreads and she leans into the Myth, **her own magic rises to match.**
- **Out of Mantle:**
  - She can't always be Mantled, especially in public venues.
  - **"Saint" gives her leeway to show small miracles of her own**, which is Myth magic, attributed to holiness.
- **She is also trained in the Concord's own spells** to keep her well-rounded.

### [Proposal] Implications
- **The Concord commits its own heresy.**
  - Mixing Myth with Mantle is the very thing it condemns (compare self-image healing).
  - Its inner circle is doing it on purpose: the Saint is a **managed heresy**, justified as the only safe way to handle Myth.
  - This is consistent with canon: they want to *control* Myth, not abolish it.
  - Real-world echo: the Inquisition kept the forbidden books it banned, and censors read everything they suppressed.
- **Dramatic irony:**
  - The Saint thinks she fooled the Concord; the impostor's household thinks it's fooling the Concord.
  - **The Saint is already managed. The impostor isn't, yet.**
  - Recognizing the same trick in each other is a powerful (and dangerous) moment.
- **What she represents strategically:**
  - Possibly the first living person who visibly bears the high god's Mantle without breaking.
  - **She is living proof of the god.** That makes her priceless to the Concord and a political lever against the Crown.
- **Scandal raises the stakes:**
  - If her Myth fails, the side effects of the eldritch Mantle **come back, possibly in public.**
  - A ruined reputation could mean an eldritch manifestation in a ballroom. This makes the triad very dangerous.
- **Who knows:**
  - Only the inner circle knows she is Myth-managed.
  - Her own confessor may sincerely believe she is simply holy.
  - **Rank-and-file Concordants who learn the truth** become a threat to the Concord itself.
- **The martyr option:**
  - A dead Saint becomes an ancestor whose Mantle can be worn, and whose story can no longer go wrong.
  - **If she strays, the Concord may decide a martyr is more useful than a living Saint.**
  - This is the quiet threat hanging over the triad ending.
- **Small miracles have to be witnessed by the right people.**
  - The Concord curates who sees what, through Hounds, the press and sermons.
  - A miracle seen by the "wrong" crowd, one that tells the story differently, could pull her Myth off course.

### [Canon] Side effects of the high god's Mantle
- **Character:** like a tribal god seen by people outside the tribe. Angry, volatile, **a force of nature pretending at civility** behind a thin veneer of holiness and generosity.
- **How it looks:** the ancestor's superposition takes **eldritch angelic features**: eyes, hands, feathers, extra limbs.
- **A fully manifested violet** is terrifying, in the vein of Lobotomy Corporation's **White Night** or Slay the Princess's **Shifting Mound**.
- **Weaker Mantles have tamer side effects:**
  - erratic mood swings;
  - strands of hair turning to feathers;
  - **wounds replaced by eyes.** The wearer can keep these hidden, and Myth reverts them over time *if no one knows they are there.*

### [Proposal] Implications
- **Why it's eldritch:** the side effects are what outsiders saw in the god.
  - Following deep-history canon (Myth flows toward the out-group), **the terror of conquered and converted peoples shaped the god's Mantle.**
  - The orthodox god was once one tribe's god. Every people it conquered added their fear of it to the Mantle.
  - Biblical echo: angels open with **"Be not afraid,"** and Ezekiel's wheels are covered in eyes.
- **Believers versus the feared:**
  - The Saint's stability depends on **believers who see benevolence outweighing the people who still fear the god.**
  - **She is least stable in borderlands and conquered provinces**, such as a border march where the god is still remembered as a conqueror.
- **The subversion of the otome heroine:**
  - The serene, pure heroine is the one secretly fighting **a divine temper.**
  - The "villainess" is the composed one.
- **Hidden eyes in a love story:**
  - An eye reverts only if no one knows about it. **A lover who sees one makes it persist.**
  - Intimacy carries a literal cost: letting someone know her body anchors the god's marks on it.
  - Trust is needed not only to share secrets but to *forget* them, or to choose not to look.
- **Gossip is a weapon:** a whispered rumour like "the Saint has an eye under her glove" could create one.
- **Feathers in a hairbrush:** a maid's discovery is the kind of small detail that could set off a Concord crisis.
- **The Concord's counter-Myth:** its sermons and press are essentially **anti-terror propaganda**, constantly writing over the outsiders' story.

## 5. Education & Debut (Historical Grounding)

**[Proposal]**
- Real 19th-century (British-style) noble upbringing: nursery (wet nurse, nanny) → governess (~6–17) with visiting masters → **presentation at court (17–18) → the Season** (London, roughly April–July, 1–3 seasons). Boys: prep school (~8) → public school (~13) → university.
- Mixed elite boarding academies are historically rare. Closest models: Japan's Gakushūin and Peeresses' School, Russia's Smolny Institute and Corps of Pages, German Ritterakademien, the Theresianum. Best period fit for an academy: **~1870–1914**.
- **The Season replaces the academy**: recurring venues, rivals, gossip, hierarchy, public humiliation. **In this world, gossip is literally magic**, so a debut is a mythmaking event. Society hostesses who control gossip (compare the patronesses of Almack's) are effectively archmages. The Lady's hag network fits this.
- If an academy exists, it's **seminary or cadet-school style under Inquisition oversight**: standardised curriculum = standardised belief. Misprinted textbooks are heresy. Duelling grounds are spaced 100 m apart.
- Religious instruction doubles as **inoculation**: "the whispers are temptation; don't listen."
- Marriage settlements are negotiated by lawyers. Childhood betrothals weren't legally binding in 19th-century Britain. A public engagement-breaking at a ball is the least realistic trope (breach-of-promise suits existed).
- Real-world parallels for martial women: samurai daughters' naginata training, Albanian **sworn virgins**, Joan of Arc.
- Real parallel for the Lord/Lady/Nurse household: the **Duke of Devonshire, Georgiana and Lady Elizabeth Foster** (ménage à trois, children raised together at Devonshire House).

## 5b. Rank, Chaplain, Hostesses, Talent & the Heroine

### [Proposal] The Lord's rank: Marquess of a border march
- *Marquess* (from *margrave*, "march lord") **originally meant a lord guarding a border.** It's the second-highest rank, high enough for a prodigy daughter to be matched with royalty or a duke's heir.
- **A border seat justifies everything:** military command in the war, a strong family Mantle, distance from the capital, and old **marcher privileges** (historical Marcher lords held their own courts). Here: **private jurisdiction over his household**, so his "treason" order of silence is literally law.
- **Two houses, two faces.** Peers sit in the upper chamber during the parliamentary session, which *is* the Season. The **town house** is clean and staffed for display; the **country seat** keeps the secrets.
- Tension: **he must feed in town.** Which children travel with him is a quiet, horrible choice.
- Alternative: **Earl/Count** if you want the match to the higher house to be a bigger reach, with the bride price explaining it.

### [Proposal] The household chaplain
- **Patronage right (*advowson*):** historically the lord nominated the parish priest and the bishop approved. The Lord picked a **pliable, ageing chaplain** who lives in the **village parsonage**, not the house. He sees Sunday services and the PR mask only. **Distance protects the secrets.**
- **The funerals:**
  - Twin killing (12y): buried as a fever death. The Nurse prepared the body.
  - **The eldest daughter's body was buried as the 1st son's.** One coffin, two lies, and the chaplain read the rites over a name that wasn't hers. Whether he suspects is open.
- **Inciting tension:** the old chaplain dies or retires around the story's start, and the Office sends a **new, young Concordant** to the town house for the Season. Purifier-leaning? He has to be handled, and his first confession with the impostor is a scene.
- [Open] Darker alternative: the chaplain is the Lord's thrall (a fed-upon, bound servant).

### [Proposal] The Patronesses
- Modelled on **Almack's Lady Patronesses** (a handful of hostesses who issued the vouchers controlling entry to the most exclusive assembly).
- Their power: **vouchers** (admission), introductions, seating, who dances with whom, and **who the Season's darling is**. **Crowning a "Diamond" literally reshapes the girl** through Myth. They may or may not know they're doing magic.
- **Clash with the Concord:** the Concord licenses *print*; the Patronesses rule the *spoken word*. **You can't license a whisper.** An uneasy truce: they can socially ruin a Concordant, and the Concord can audit them.
- **Factions:**
  - **Old Guard:** lineage, true names, ancestor pedigree
  - **Reformers / new money:** wealth, industry, "borrowed hands" accepted
  - **Martial hostesses:** wives and widows of generals scouting talent
  - **Hidden:** some are Myth-touched; **the Lady's hag network overlaps the Patronesses** or wants a seat among them
- Possible titles: "the Patronesses," "the Assembly," "the Lady Vouchers."

### [Proposal] The draw of military talent
- Every battalion needs a **rotation of Mantle-bearers**, so armies, houses and orders constantly **scout violets**. It works like a sports draft crossed with horse breeding.
- **The Season includes exhibition duels**, held on great lawns or parks with 100 m spacing. They're showcases for talent and bride price.
- **Routes up for commoners:** sponsorship, adoption, knighthood or barony (the 2nd bastard's ambition), or marrying into a house.
- **Competing recruiters:** the Crown / War Office, Concord battle-orders, great houses (via marriage), Patronesses (matchmaking), mercenary companies, merchant houses (privateers).

## 5c. The Season: Structure & Stations of Canon

### Historical reference (Victorian London, ~1840–1900)
- **Timing:** follows the sitting of Parliament. Roughly March to July, peaking May–July. It ends around **12 August** (the "Glorious Twelfth," start of grouse shooting), when society leaves for the country: **autumn house parties**, then winter hunting.
- **Preparation:** dozens of gowns; court dress (white, a long train, ostrich feathers, a veil); dancing and curtsey lessons; securing a sponsor.
- **Presentation at court:** afternoon "Drawing Rooms" where the debutante curtseys to the Queen (peers' daughters were kissed on the cheek), then backs out without tripping on her train. After that she is **"out."**
- **Regular rhythm:** morning calls and **card-leaving** (15-minute visits; card etiquette is its own language), afternoon rides and drives on **Rotten Row** in Hyde Park, evenings of dinners, opera boxes and several balls a night.
- **Fixed events:** Royal Academy Summer Exhibition (May), the Derby (early June), **Royal Ascot** (June; the Royal Enclosure needed vouchers), Henley Regatta and Eton v Harrow at Lord's (July), Cowes Week (August).
- **Rules:**
  - Always chaperoned; no dancing without an introduction.
  - **Dance cards.** Refusing one man means sitting out that dance. More than two or three dances with the same man signals an engagement.
  - Proposals go to the father; then lawyers negotiate settlements.
- **Cost:** ruinous. Families went into debt for a daughter's Season.

### [Proposal] This world's Season: stations of canon
**Prelude (before arrival)**
1. **Concord certificate:** catechism review and **confession with the household chaplain**.
2. **Ancestor registry review:** house epithets and approved ancestors checked by the heraldic college.
3. **Voucher application to the Patronesses.**
4. **Wardrobe:** the Couturier, gown-keeping seals, complexion tonics.

**Opening**
5. **Presentation at court:** a curtsey before the Sovereign, **the only Mantled person in the throne room**. Every debutante feels the royal Mantle's pressure as she bows. The herald announces her with her **house epithet**, a formal public naming.
6. **The Benediction:** a Concord mass blessing the year's debutantes. Hounds attend.
7. **The Patronesses' opening assembly:** the first voucher-only ball. **The Diamond of the Season is named** here or soon after, and Myth starts working on her.

**Middle**
8. **Calls, cards, rides, opera, dinners**: the daily grind of gossip and visibility.
9. **The Lists:** exhibition duels and displays on great lawns spaced 100 m apart. **Colours are revealed.** For a prodigy-priced bride, **a public showing is expected at least once.**
10. **Parliamentary sessions:** the Lord sits; the Commoner Mantle Restriction Act and the Press Licensing Act are debated; ladies watch from the gallery.
11. **Racing, regatta and the Exhibition:** the Academy's portrait exhibition, where **a portrait shapes belief** about its subject.
12. **The Concord feast day:** a midseason procession, the living Saint on display.
13. **Banns and contract sealing:** engagements are announced, banns read, contracts magically sealed.

**Close**
14. **The Sovereign's ball:** final assembly; last chances.
15. **Exodus to the country:** autumn house parties continue courtships away from the Patronesses' eyes. **Riskier for the manor, if guests visit the country seat.**

**In-world rules:**
- **No Mantling in ballrooms** except the host.
- A dance card with too many Mantled partners is gossip-worthy.
- Chaperones are often Mantle-trained.
- Three dances with the same man = an understanding.
- **An already-betrothed debutante still does the Season**, but for her it's an **audition to *keep* the contract**: she is evaluated by the fiancé's family, displayed beside him, and compared against rivals such as the heroine.

### Fates of the unmatched
**Historical:**
- Another Season; **two or three was normal**, and after that she was "on the shelf." By about 25, a spinster.
- **The "Fishing Fleet":** young women sent to British India to find husbands among officers and officials. Those who failed were called "returned empties."
- **Marrying down:** a curate, an officer, or new money (rank traded for wealth).
- **Genteel dependency:** companion to a relative, the spinster aunt, a governess (the genteel poverty trap).
- **Convent** (in Catholic countries), charity, travel.
- Men had more time: younger sons went to the army, the Church or the colonies.

**[Proposal] In-world:**
- **Prove your colour.** An unmatched girl spends a year training to show a higher colour next Season and raise her value.
- **The Concord sisterhood:** nursing orders (Plague Saint), cloisters, battle-sisters. A refuge, a threat, or a career.
- **The weapon path:** **unmatched violets are recruited by the army or the Crown.** For a strong woman, failing the marriage market can open a commission instead. This directly serves the "woman as weapon" theme.
- **Border garrisons as the Fishing Fleet:** marcher and colonial forts where officers need wives.
- **Marrying down to new money:** the balancing payment flows toward her rank.
- **The hag network** quietly recruits "ruined" or overlooked girls. Sinister.

## 5d. The Lists: Exchanges, Worth & Refined Brutality

### [Canon] Premise
- The Lists are **not one event.** They are a Season-long series of exchanges, meant to reveal every side of a participant over time, or to give them chances to show themselves off.
- **Colour is only one part of a person's worth.** A Blue can be earned through skill, understanding or devotion. How well someone *wields* a Mantle matters as much as how deep it goes.
- **There is tension with family secrets.** A family is expected to show "enough" and little more.
- **Youths, especially young men, are eager to test themselves.** "Refined brutality" has a cultural appeal of its own.

### Historical precedents
- **Mensur** (German student fencing, 1800s–today): ritualized bouts with no winner, judged on composure under the blade. The duelling scar (*Schmiss*) was a mark of class and was considered attractive. This is the clearest example of "refined brutality."
- **Pas d'armes** (15th c.): a knight held a passage and challenged anyone who came, performed in front of ladies.
- **Japanese *taryū-jiai* and *dōjō-yaburi*:** challenges between rival schools. Secret techniques were passed on only orally (*kuden*), and the master's licence (*menkyo kaiden*) meant full transmission. Showing your school's real technique to an outsider was a betrayal.
- **Fencing salles and boxing under the Queensberry rules:** Victorian gentlemen sparring in clubs as a social activity.
- **The Victorian ideal of the gentleman amateur:** showing visible effort was vulgar. The best display looked effortless and held something back.

### [Proposal] The four facets of worth
| Facet | Cultivation equivalent | What it shows | How it's assessed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Colour** (Depth) | cultivation realm | How strongly the ancestor answers | Seen the moment someone Mantles |
| **Command** (Wielding) | technique mastery, combat insight | Fighting skill, timing, use of ghost limbs and pressure | Bouts and exchanges |
| **Breadth** (Range) | multiple arts / dual cultivation | Several Mantles at a competent level. **The Change**, swapping Mantles quickly and cleanly within the strain limit, is a skill to show off. | Demonstrations, relay bouts |
| **Reading** (Lore) | knowing other sects' techniques | Knowing opposing Mantles: how to match them, counter them or avoid them | Strategy games, commentary, and holding your own against unfamiliar archetypes |

- A "green who fights like a blue" is a recognized and respected type: **the technician.**
- A "blue who fights like a green" is a well-known disappointment: someone who **wears the ancestor but doesn't carry them.**

### [Proposal] Vocabulary (cultivation terms → this world)
| Cultivation term | In-world term | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Trading pointers | **Crossing colours** / an **exchange** | A friendly sparring bout, nominally to teach each other |
| Challenging a sect | **Calling the passage** | A formal public challenge |
| Concealing cultivation | **Keeping reserve** / "wearing it low" | Showing less than you have. **This is good manners.** |
| Face-slapping | **Unhorsing** | A humiliating public defeat of someone arrogant |
| Hidden expert | **A still water** | Someone underestimated who turns out formidable |
| Sect secret technique | **House rote** / **closed rite** | A family technique never shown in public |
| Breakthrough | **Deepening** | Gaining a shade of colour |
| Comprehension | **Hearing the ancestor** | The insight that grows Command |

### [Proposal] Kinds of exchange
- **Lawn exchanges:** casual bouts at garden parties, with lawns 100 m apart to keep pressure from overlapping. Ladies watch, and men show off.
- **Salle bouts:** club sparring. Men only, at least officially.
- **Called passages:** formal challenges, with seconds and a herald. Turning one down costs reputation, unless you have a good excuse.
- **Readings:** parlour strategy games played with archetype cards, plus "lore duels" over dinner. This is where ladies and staff officers shine.
- **The Hunt:** Mantled hunting at autumn house parties. It shows Breadth and stamina, away from the Patronesses.
- **The Grand Passage:** one formal showcase late in the Season, with the Sovereign present. Prodigies are expected to appear.
- **Ladies:**
  - Ordinary demonstrations are decorous, using craft and household Mantles.
  - Women of the weapon path cross colours openly. **A mixed bout is scandalous, and very popular.**

### [Proposal] Etiquette of reserve
- **Asking someone which ancestor they wear is rude**, and asking for a true name is unforgivable. People read your colour and archetype from what you show.
- **"Enough":**
  - Show the house's public Mantle at a respectable colour, with competent Command.
  - Hold back your second and third Mantles and your house rotes.
  - **Showing everything is vulgar**, like discussing your income. It also hands rival houses your counters.
- **Pressing someone** (provoking them until they reveal more than they meant to) is a recognized, ungentlemanly tactic. It is still used constantly.

### [Proposal] Refined brutality
- Mantled wounds heal on revive, so violence feels cheap, and young men treat it that way. The brutality is part of the show.
- **What counts as refined:**
  - Composure.
  - Restraint at the right moment.
  - Never breaking the "no revives" convention in casual bouts. **Forcing an opponent to revive is a grave insult**, because it spends one of their lives.
- **The bare scar:**
  - A wound taken *unmantled*, which the ancestors won't mend, is the in-world *Schmiss*.
  - Fighting bare shows nerve. Officially it's disreputable; in practice, women find it attractive.
  - It brings real risk of maiming and death back into play.
- **Concord view:** they tolerate it, and use it to vent young men's energy that might otherwise turn to Myth. **But a crowd chanting a champion's name is gossip, and gossip is Myth.** Hounds attend the big passages.

### [Canon] Challenge etiquette: the direction matters
- **Women can call a passage.** Gender is secondary to Mantle strength.
- **Strong challenging weak:** allowed, because power favours whoever acts, but it looks **bullish.** You win the bout and lose the room.
- **Weak challenging strong:** **honourable**, *if* it's done with humility and proper etiquette. The crowd roots for the underdog even if everyone expects the stronger one to win.
- **Women are read as the weaker party by default.** That changes only if:
  - she makes clear through **dress or behaviour** that she is a warrior first and a damsel second, or
  - she proves she can back up real strength **while presenting as a delicate flower** the rest of the time. This is the most dangerous and most admired kind.

### [Proposal] How it plays out
- **The form of the challenge is everything.**
  - A humble challenge is a request to *learn*: "Would you do me the honour of correcting my form?"
  - An arrogant one is a demand.
  - The same bout can make or break a reputation depending on which was used.
- **The strong side's options:**
  - **Accept graciously and teach:** the crowd approves.
  - **Decline gracefully:** acceptable, but it costs a little.
  - **Crush the challenger:** a hollow win that looks bullish.
- **The underdog's prize:** the challenger who loses well can gain more than the winner. **Losing well is a recognized social achievement.**
- **The delicate-flower ambush:** a woman who has presented as soft all Season and then calls a passage and wins causes an **unhorsing** and a sensation. The impostor and Tulpa could use exactly this.

### [Proposal] Softer men: Civil and Martial standing
**Historical precedents:**
- **China's *wen/wu* ideal:** civil-scholarly versus martial masculinity, with ***wen* often ranked higher.** The scholar-official, not the general, was the model gentleman.
- **Heian Japan:** courtiers were judged on poetry, calligraphy, perfume and taste in colours.
- **The Regency dandy** (Beau Brummell) and the **aesthete** (Wilde); the 18th-century **"man of feeling."**
- **Prince Albert:** the consort who ran the household, the estate and policy while the Queen held the power.

**In-world:**
- **Two recognized standings for men *and* women: Martial and Civil.**
  - **Martial:** soldiers and weapons. Expected to cross colours.
  - **Civil:** scholars, Licentiates, healers, craft Mantles, diplomats, stewards, clergy.
- **Signalled by dress**, so nobody has to ask:
  - Martial wear a sword-knot, sash or cut of coat; civil wear something else.
  - Women's weapon-path dress mixes in martial elements.
- **Civil men are exempt from bullish challenges.** Calling a passage on a civil man is bullying, and the crowd will turn on you.
  - A civil man may still choose to accept, and **a civil man winning is the biggest underdog story there is.**
- **There are fashionable softer roles** (aesthetic, scholarly and devout), each with its own salons. Being Civil is respectable, not effeminate.
- **The market logic:**
  - **Weapon-women need Civil husbands.** The army takes her away; someone has to run the estate, raise the heirs, manage the money and host. That makes him **the steward-husband**, a respected Albert-style role.
  - **Martial men often prefer Civil wives**, following traditional household roles.
  - **Two Martials marrying** is romantic, an "iron match," but considered impractical: who keeps the house?
  - **[Canon] Two Civils marrying** is the ordinary, traditional household, acceptable wherever no border threatens. A Martial bride bought for a Civil heir is the *premium* match; if she turns out Civil, the match still stands, but the price paid now looks too high.
- **The Concord** is quietly pro-Civil, since scholars and clergy are its recruiting ground. It treats Martial excess as a Myth risk.
- **Changing standing is possible but carries a stigma:**
  - A man who "hangs up the knot" after an injury or a bad defeat may be pitied.

## 7. Glossary & Terminology

**Naming is a magical act.** Words shape belief, and belief shapes Myth. Which term someone uses for a Myth-touched person pushes that person toward monstrosity or toward mildness, so terminology is a quiet battlefield between the faith, folk custom and the Myth-born themselves.

### [Canon]
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Myth-touched** | General, neutral term for anyone or anything shaped by Myth |
| **Mystique** | Someone who *deliberately* embraces Myth |
| **Fallen / Warped / Cursed / Witch / Demon** | Orthodox condemnations of the Myth-touched |
| **Wild Magic** | Unsanctioned Myth (vs sanctioned formal magic) |

### [Proposal] Orthodox / Inquisition
- **Creatures**: the official term for all Myth-born, even humanoid ones. Denies their personhood on purpose.
- **Tainted / Marked**: humans showing Myth traits without having fallen.
- **Inquisition internal (technical) terms**, used by those who know the truth:
  - **Afflicted**: an individual still mid-transformation (unstable, reversible?)
  - **Wrought**: a being believed into existence in one lifetime (first generation)
  - **Settled / Lineal**: Myth-born lines whose nature has stabilised into natural law (no longer dependent on Myth)
  - **Ledgered**: known, registered Myth-touched people tolerated under watch

### [Proposal] Folk / commoner
- **Euphemisms are deliberate Myth hygiene.** Like the historical "Fair Folk" or "Good Neighbours," commoners use flattering or vague names to avoid feeding fear. Village custom: never call a thing by its fear-name. **The Inquisition dislikes euphemisms** (they soften orthodox doctrine) but tolerates them because they work.
- **Changeling**: a child who came out "wrong" or uncanny.
- **Fetch**: an uncanny double (Irish folklore). **This is what servants would whisper about the twins**, and later about the identical 4th daughter.

### [Proposal] Nobility
- **Storied blood / an old story**: polite hints that a house is Myth-touched. Said with pride among peers, never in front of clergy.
- **The house's likeness**: a family resemblance that's *too* strong (the twins).

### [Proposal] Myth-born self-terms
- **The Storied** or **the Told**: neutral self-name; "we are what was told of us."
- **Retold / Recast**: those who reformed their nature through deliberate PR or a lifestyle change (the firebreather who became a firebender). Distinct from Mystique, since they no longer *pull* on Myth; they *steer* it.
- **Kindred**: among themselves, regardless of species.
- **Passers / the Lightly Told**: those who can pass as human.
- [Open] Do different species have their own names for themselves (dragons, fae, hags)?

### [Proposal] Vocabulary of sanctioned magic
**Principle:** "magic" means the unknown, so the Concord avoids it. Sanctioned workings are named like **liturgy, medicine and craft**: things that are *known, repeatable and taught*.

**The whole**
| Term | Register | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **The Canon** / **canonical arts** | Official | The approved list; anything else is **apocryphal** |
| **The Ordained Arts** | Formal, polite society | "She's well versed in the Arts" |
| **Natural philosophy** | Scholarly | Real historical term for science; implies understanding, not mystery |
| **Rote-work** | Common | Slightly dismissive: "just rote" |
| **Magic** | Vulgar / suspect | Implies the unknown. Polite people avoid it; "**Wild Magic**" is the legal term for the illicit |

**Branches: "Disciplines"**
- Formal: the Discipline of Physic (medicine), of the Hearth (household), of Arms (military), of Seals (law, trade, inscription), of Order (Concord rites).
- Common names: **physic, hearthwork, warcraft, sealwork, churchwork**.

**Single workings: named by type**
| Term | Meaning | Real-world root |
|---|---|---|
| **Rote** | A spoken or gestured working learned by memory | "Learned by rote" |
| **Receipt** | A brewed or mixed preparation (draughts, poultices, salts) | Historical spelling of *recipe*; household "receipt books" |
| **Draught / physic / tincture** | Specific receipt forms | Apothecary terms |
| **Rite** | Multi-step or multi-person ceremony (Shriving, oath-binding) | Liturgy |
| **Seal / mark** | An inscribed or enchanted object (wards, licence seals, cold-boxes) | Preferred over "charm" or "enchantment" |
| **Operation** | Scholarly or legal term for any working | Alchemical usage |

**Theology: form and matter.** Borrowed from sacramental theology: every canonical working has a **form** (words, gesture, inscription) and **matter** (materials, the subject). A working is **valid** if form and matter are correct; whether it's *effective* is another question. That's a perfect excuse for madhouse scholarly quarrels.

**Practitioners**
- **Licentiate**: a licensed practitioner (compare the real Licentiate of the Society of Apothecaries).
- **Scholar**: a student of the Arts.
- **Hedge-** prefix: unlicensed (hedge-witch, hedge-physic).

**Class register** (one word, three speakers)
| Noble / formal | Commoner | Concord / courts |
|---|---|---|
| a rote, a receipt | a charm, a cure, a trick | an operation |
| the Arts | rote-work | the Canon |
| an apocryphal variant | grandmother's way | an irregularity |
| (never) "spell" | a spell (casual) | a spell = **evidence of Wild Magic** |

- **"Spell" and "charm" are folk words the Concord tolerates as euphemism** but which sound suspect in court. A noble saying "spell" sounds vulgar or sinister.
- **"Apocryphal"** is the polite middle ground: a folk variant of a canonical rote. Not quite illegal; not quite safe.
